What a stack test is

A stack test, also called a source test, emissions test, or performance test, measures the pollutants a stationary source releases through a stack or duct. A test crew samples the exhaust at specific points inside the stack, while the facility runs its process at agreed conditions. The result is a concentration, a gas flow rate, and an emission rate that can be compared with a limit.

Facilities test because something requires it or because they need the numbers:

  • Permit limits. Air permits set emission limits and usually require testing to show the source meets them, once after startup and often periodically after that.
  • Federal standards. New Source Performance Standards (NSPS, 40 CFR Part 60) and National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP, 40 CFR Parts 61 and 63) require performance tests for the source categories they cover.
  • Control device performance. Testing at the inlet and outlet of a baghouse, scrubber, or oxidizer shows its removal or destruction efficiency.
  • Monitor certification. Continuous emission monitoring systems (CEMS) are certified and audited against reference method measurements in a relative accuracy test audit (RATA).
  • Engineering data. Emission factors, permit applications, troubleshooting, and design of new controls.

Because the result can decide whether a facility is in compliance, every step follows a written, standardized procedure. The procedures are EPA’s reference methods, and the rest of this course teaches them.

Who is involved

The facility
Owns the source and the obligation to test. Runs the process at the required conditions, records process data for each run, and provides safe ports, platforms, access, and power.
The test contractor
The stack testing crew. Writes the test protocol, calibrates equipment, runs the sampling, recovers samples, does the calculations, and writes the report.
The regulatory agency
The state, local, or EPA regional office that receives the notification and report. It may approve the test plan and send an observer to watch the runs.
The laboratory
Analyzes samples that cannot be finished in the field: gravimetric weighing, ion chromatography, metals, organics. Often in-house at the test company.

Where the rules come from

Two kinds of rules shape a test. The applicable standard or permit says what to measure, the limit, its units, how long each run must be, and the operating conditions. The reference methods say how to measure it. Both live in Title 40 of the Code of Federal Regulations.

The general provisions

Each NSPS and NESHAP has general provisions that set the ground rules for testing. For NSPS (40 CFR 60.8), the main points are:

  • The initial performance test happens within 60 days of reaching the maximum production rate, and no later than 180 days after initial startup.
  • The facility notifies the agency at least 30 days before the test, so an observer can attend.
  • The test runs under conditions that represent the source’s performance, as the standard or the agency specifies. The facility documents those conditions.
  • A test is normally three separate runs, and compliance is judged on the average of the three.
  • The facility provides sampling ports, safe platforms, safe access to them, and utilities.

NESHAP general provisions (40 CFR 63.7) follow the same pattern with tighter planning: typically a longer notice period, and a site-specific test plan the agency can ask to review and approve before the test. Reports are commonly due within 60 days of the test. Subparts and permits can override any of these, so always read the specific rule that applies.

Read the subpart first

The individual standard often changes the defaults: minimum run times or sample volumes, required methods, operating limits set during the test, or the oxygen level results must be corrected to. A test run correctly by the method but in the wrong conditions still fails.

The test lifecycle

A day on the platform

A typical compliance test day starts before sunrise and runs three test runs back to back. The crew splits: some work on the platform at the sampling ports, some work in the mobile lab or trailer at the base of the stack running analyzers and recovering samples. The facility keeps the process steady the whole time.

The process is part of the test

If the unit trips, the load drops, or the control device misbehaves during a run, the samples no longer represent the required conditions. Crews pause sampling, note the time, and agree with the facility and observer whether to continue or repeat the run. Process data logged during every run is what proves the conditions were right.

How the methods fit together

The reference methods are not independent tests; each one feeds the next. Below they are in dependency order. This course follows the method numbers, but notice that Method 2 needs results from Methods 3 and 4 before it can produce a velocity. The flow map on the home page shows the same chain as a diagram.

  1. Method 1: traverse pointsPicks the sampling location and the points inside the stack. Every other method samples at those points.
  2. Method 3: gas compositionMeasures O₂ and CO₂ to get the dry molecular weight of the gas.
  3. Method 4: moistureMeasures the water vapor fraction. With Method 3 it gives the wet molecular weight, and it converts wet volumes to a dry basis.
  4. Method 2: velocity and flow rateTurns pitot readings into velocity using the molecular weight from Methods 3 and 4, then into a dry standard flow rate.
  5. Method 5: particulate matterUses all of the above to sample isokinetically, then reports a concentration and, with the flow rate, an emission rate.
  6. Instrumental methodsMeasure gaseous pollutants continuously. Their concentrations combine with the Method 2 flow rate the same way.

In practice Methods 2, 3, and 4 run at the same time as Method 5: the pitot tube is mounted on the probe, the impingers in the Method 5 train collect the moisture, and a bag or analyzer collects the gas composition sample.

Safety on the platform

Stack sampling platforms can be well over a hundred feet up, next to hot steel, on a stack full of combustion gas. Most test companies run a job safety analysis at the start of every day. The hazards to plan for:

HazardWhat it looks likeTypical controls
FallsClimbing fixed ladders with equipment, open platform edges, gaps around portsFall protection on ladders and unguarded edges, hoists for equipment instead of carrying it, intact guardrails and toe boards
Falling objectsTools, probes, and glassware dropped from the platformBarricade the area below, tool lanyards, toe boards, hard hats on the ground crew
Heat and hot surfacesStack walls, ports, and probes withdrawn from a hot stack; summer heat on the platformHeat-resistant gloves, probe rests, hydration and rest breaks
Stack gas exposureGas blowing out of an open port on a positive-pressure duct: CO, SO₂, H₂S, low O₂Know the duct pressure before opening ports, stand upwind, personal gas monitors, port seals
ElectricalHeaters, pumps, and long extension cords in wet weatherGround-fault protection, inspected cords, cords kept clear of hot surfaces and walkways
WeatherLightning, high wind, iceLeave the platform when lightning is near; stop work in high wind or icing conditions

Before the first run, confirm

  • The applicable limits, methods, run times, and operating conditions are agreed and written in the test plan.
  • The agency was notified on time, and you know whether an observer is coming.
  • Equipment calibrations are current and the records travel with the crew.
  • The facility knows the schedule and who records process data for each run.
  • The platform, access, and power were checked during the site survey.
