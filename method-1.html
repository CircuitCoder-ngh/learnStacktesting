Why one point is never enough

Gas does not move up a stack as a uniform column. Friction slows it near the walls, so the center runs faster than the edges. Anything that bends, narrows, widens, or stirs the flow—an elbow, a fan, a damper, a scrubber inlet—pushes the fast-moving core off to one side, and it takes several diameters of straight duct for the flow to settle again.

Pollutant concentration can be uneven too, especially for particulate, where heavy particles get flung to the outside of bends. A single measurement at one spot can therefore be badly wrong. Method 1 solves this two ways: it pushes the sampling location far from disturbances, and it spreads measurements over a grid of points that each represent an equal share of the cross-section.

Choosing the measurement site

Method 1 measures distances in stack diameters, so the same rule works on a 2-foot vent and a 20-foot power plant stack. Two distances matter:

  • Distance B runs from the nearest disturbance upstream of the port (where the gas came from) to the port. The flow needs this length to recover.
  • Distance A runs from the port to the nearest disturbance downstream (where the gas is going), often the stack exit. Flow starts to rearrange itself before it reaches an obstacle.

The ideal site is at least 8 diameters downstream and 2 diameters upstream of any disturbance: the “8-and-2 rule.” A disturbance is anything that changes the flow—bends, expansions, contractions, fans, dampers, control devices, or a visible flame. Meet 8-and-2 and you sample the minimum number of points. Fall short and you need more points, read from Figure 1-1 of the method. Below 2 diameters downstream or 0.5 diameters upstream the site is unacceptable without the regulator’s approval.

Minimum points when the 8-and-2 rule is met

  • 12 points for circular or rectangular stacks wider than 24 in. (0.61 m)
  • 8 points for circular stacks 12 to 24 in. (0.30–0.61 m)
  • 9 points for rectangular stacks with an equivalent diameter of 12 to 24 in.

Method 1 applies to stacks larger than 12 in. diameter (or 113 in.² area). Smaller ducts, from 4 to 12 in., use Method 1A. Velocity-only traverses can use Figure 1-2, which allows fewer points than Figure 1-1.

Locating the points: equal areas

Each traverse point must represent the same share of the cross-sectional area, so that a simple average of the readings is a true area-weighted average. For a circular stack, Method 1 divides the circle into concentric rings of equal area and places each point at the radius that splits its ring into two equal halves. Two traverse diameters, at right angles through two ports, cover the stack.

Because the outer rings are thin and the inner ones are wide, the points crowd toward the walls. That is deliberate: the wall region holds a lot of area even though it looks narrow.

Keeping points off the wall

On small stacks the outermost points can land so close to the wall that the nozzle or pitot would touch it. Method 1 moves them:

  • Stacks wider than 24 in.: no point closer than 1.0 in. to the wall, or the nozzle inside diameter if that is larger.
  • Stacks 24 in. or smaller: no point closer than 0.5 in., or the nozzle inside diameter if larger.

If two neighboring points collapse onto the same adjusted location, you still treat them as two separate points: sample there twice as long and record two readings.

Checking for cyclonic flow

Methods 2 and 5 assume the gas moves straight up the stack. Downstream of cyclones, tangential inlets, wet scrubbers, and some fans, the gas can instead spiral. A pitot tube aimed straight up would then read the wrong velocity, and a nozzle facing straight down would sample at the wrong angle.

The check uses the Type S pitot tube in a sideways position, called the 0° reference: the planes of the two openings are perpendicular to the stack cross-section, so gas moving straight up flows across both openings equally and the manometer reads zero (a “null”). If the gas is swirling, it hits one opening harder and the manometer reads something. You rotate the pitot until the reading returns to zero; the rotation angle \alpha is the flow’s yaw angle at that point.

Cyclonic flow criterion

\bar{\alpha} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_i\rvert \;\le\; 20^\circ

Acceptance criteria

Method 1 at a glance

  • Stack diameter greater than 12 in. (0.30 m), or cross-section greater than 113 in.² (0.071 m²). Otherwise use Method 1A.
  • Ideal site: at least 8 diameters downstream and 2 diameters upstream of any disturbance.
  • Absolute minimum: 2 diameters downstream and 0.5 diameters upstream.
  • Particulate traverses: up to 24 points (circular) or 25 (rectangular) from Figure 1-1.
  • Points no closer to the wall than 1.0 in. (stacks over 24 in.) or 0.5 in. (24 in. and under), or the nozzle inside diameter if larger.
  • Rectangular elements with a length-to-width ratio between 1.0 and 2.0.
  • Average absolute yaw angle no more than 20°.

Field mistakes that cost runs

Measuring from the wrong reference

Point distances in Table 1-2 are from the inside wall, but you mark the probe from the outside face of the port. Forget to add the nipple length and wall thickness and every point shifts toward the far wall.

Forgetting the stack exit

The top of the stack is a disturbance. A port 1.5 diameters below the exit fails the 2-diameter upstream criterion even if the inlet is far below.

Trusting the drawings

Measure the inside diameter at the ports. Stacks get relined, refractory erodes, and some are not round. Check the distance to the far wall through both ports.

Skipping the cyclonic check after a scrubber

Wet scrubbers, cyclones, and tangential inlets are classic sources of swirl. Run the null check before you commit to the location, not after three runs.
